## 1. Research-first principle {#research-first}
> **Status:** draft · standalones + LinkedIn

**Standalone A — stop / do** *(§9)*

Don't start with the mood board — it only shows you what looks nice.

What "modern" means for a CFO moving millions comes from research, not references. Do that first, and the board becomes evidence for a decision, not the decision itself.

> ✅ posted 2026-07-02 (manual, X)

---

**Standalone B — what actually made the difference** *(§10)*

My boss gave me a one-line brief: "make the demo more modern."

When he pushed back on the first pass, I had a reason for every choice — the type, the density, the restraint. That's what a few hours of research buys you: not a nicer screen, an answer for every "why is it like that?"

---

**Standalone C — old way → new way** *(§3)*

How I start a redesign now:

Old way: open Figma, pull Dribbble shots, start vibing.
New way: desk research, form a point of view, *then* the mood board.

A point of view turns references into trade-offs. Vibing turns them into a taste vote.

---

**Standalone D — the comparison** *(§8)*

A point of view > a mood board.

One makes the mood board a choice between trade-offs. The other makes it a contest the loudest person wins.

---

**Standalone E — setup → payoff → kicker** *(§11)*

A few hours across twelve sources. Before I touched Figma.

To turn a taste contest into a design decision. Looks like the long way round. It's the shortcut.

---

**Standalone F — what "modern" is** *(§17)*

"Make it more modern" is not a visual request.

It's asking whether the product looks like it understands the year its users are working in. That's a research question before it's a design one.

---

**Standalone G — AI fills the gap** *(§17 → observation)*

If you don't define what your product stands for before you build, the AI defines it for you.

It reaches for its defaults. Its defaults are everyone else's defaults. That's how you ship the same product as the team next to you.

---

**Standalone H — the brief before the brief** *(§12 simple-advice)*

Before you pick a single font, write down:

— the typographic posture
— the gradient policy
— the dark-mode stance
— the motion vocabulary

The things people used to skip defining are now the whole difference.

---

**Standalone I — reacting vs using** *(§8 comparison)*

Using references > reacting to references.

Without a point of view, a mood board is a wall of things you like. With one, it's evidence for a decision you can defend.

---

### LinkedIn post (~200 words)

My boss handed me a one-line brief: "make the demo more modern." No persona, no constraints, no guidance.

Before I touched Figma, I spent a few hours on research instead. Here's why that order matters.

Skip the research and you react to references instead of using them. The mood board becomes a vote on taste. Someone picks the one they like. The loudest person in the room wins. That's a preference, not a decision.

Form a point of view first and the conversation changes. You're not picking what looks nice — you're choosing between trade-offs. This direction earns its keep for our user. That one doesn't, and here's why.

The research also surfaced something worth saying plainly: speed is now the baseline for anything AI-assisted. Every tool produces fast. What's left is identity — the part the AI can't fill in, because it's a story it doesn't have access to yet. Skip the research and the AI reaches for its defaults. Every product ends up with the same finish.

The more context I have before I build, the more defensible every choice downstream becomes.

If you're staring at a vague brief: don't open the mood board first. Open the research.

## 2. Patterns not pixels {#patterns-not-pixels}
> **Status:** draft · standalones + LinkedIn

**Standalone A — the comparison** *(§8)*

Brand is the patterns, not the pixels.

The AI can compose the screen. It can't compose what the screen always *does*.

---

**Standalone B — the equation** *(§7)*

Brand identity when the AI builds the screen = fixed slots + a defined motion vocabulary + the parts the AI isn't allowed to touch.

Everything else can be generated per user.

---

**Standalone C — the breakdown** *(§14)*

AI slop is 10% the model and 90% no one telling it what has to stay fixed.

No slot structure. No motion rule. No "you can compose X, you can't touch Y." So it composes everything, and everything drifts.

---

**Standalone D — every role should understand** *(§13)*

Every designer shipping with AI should understand:

— the patterns travel; the pixels don't
— page actions live in one fixed corner, always
— high-stakes confirmations carry the same beat, always
— the AI fills the slot; it doesn't move the slot

---

**Standalone E — the CFO** *(§17 → concrete)*

A CFO moving millions doesn't care about your bouncy onboarding animation.

Trust is the interface behaving the same way every time, under the same circumstances. Predictable patterns. Chrome that gets out of the way.

---

**Standalone F — old way → new way** *(§3)*

The designer's question, updated:

Old: what should this screen look like?
New: what stays invariant, no matter what the AI builds?

The second is harder. It needs a system the AI can read, not a Figma file full of hex codes.

---

**Standalone G — where unique products come from** *(§9)*

Stop trying to out-polish the AI screen by screen.

Define the slots, the motion, and the untouchables once — then let it compose inside them. Leave no gaps and the AI works for you. Leave every gap and it works instead of you.

---

**Standalone H — navigation → intent** *(§3 old→new)*

UI used to be navigation: click through menus to find the thing.

UI in 2026 is intent: the interface recognizes what you're trying to do and brings the tools forward. The fixed slot structure is what makes that possible without breaking the user's spatial memory.

---

**Standalone I — the constraints brief** *(§6 N-things+kicker)*

The brief a designer writes now is a constraints brief:

— what the AI composes freely
— what stays fixed
— what the user can override
— what gets announced vs done silently

That's the design work. The pixels come after.

---

### LinkedIn post (~210 words)

If generative UI gets good enough that no two users see the same layout, what is brand identity?

The easy answer is logo, colors, fonts. But those are the surface — and the first things AI learns to replicate generically.

The answer I came back to: the brand is the patterns, not the pixels.

Page actions always in the top-right corner. High-stakes confirmations always carrying a deliberate processing beat. AI suggestions always in the margin, never overwriting content. Those patterns hold across every generated screen. The content inside them can be entirely user-specific.

That's what design work looks like when AI is building parts of your product: you define what's invariant. The slot structure. The motion vocabulary. What the AI can compose freely, and what it can't touch.

Products that don't read as AI slop are the ones that made those calls early. They wrote the constraints before they opened the tools. They gave the AI a brief detailed enough to do the job without filling gaps generically.

The designer who did that work isn't less relevant because AI builds screens now. They're the reason the screens are defensible.

If your product looks AI-shaped, the fix usually isn't more polish. It's deciding what has to stay fixed.

## 3. The vague brief {#vague-brief}
> **Status:** draft · Twitter-only (LI angle covered by Idea 1)

**Standalone A — one-sentence opener** *(§17)*

"Make it more modern" isn't a brief — it's an argument the team keeps having because no one defined it.

Nobody wrote down what "modern" means here, so every review reopens it. Have that conversation once, before Figma. Skip it and the mood board becomes a vote on taste.

---

**Standalone B — stop / do** *(§9)*

Stop executing the vague brief as written.

Find out what the one-liner was actually asking for. "More modern" is a proxy for "something isn't matching what we claim to be" — design that, not the sentence.

---

**Standalone C — the comparison** *(§8)*

"What does modern mean for a B2B advisor product in 2026?" > "what looks modern right now?"

One has an answer. The other has a winner.

---

**Standalone D — taste vs principles** *(§14)*

At a small consultancy with no written design language, most decisions are 100% taste.

The boss likes a serif this week, something else last month. That holds with one product and one decision-maker. It breaks at five.

---

**Standalone E — the research move** *(§11)*

Twelve sources. A few hours. Before the mood board.

Not to become an expert — to walk in with a point of view instead of a blank page. Seems like the long way. It ends the taste argument before it starts.

---

**Standalone F — what I brought back** *(§6)*

What the research gave me for a one-line brief:

— a name for what "modern" means for *this* product
— a reason to pick one direction that isn't "I think it looks better"
— a brief my boss could actually push back on

Specific enough to argue with beats persuasive.

---

**Standalone G — the diagnostic read** *(§8 comparison)*

A vague brief tells you the team's stage.

One product, one decision-maker: taste works fine. Five products, five stakeholders: taste breaks and you feel it as "make it more modern." The brief is the symptom, not the task.

## 4. The delay rule {#delay-rule}
> **Status:** draft · Twitter-only (tactical register)

**Standalone A — the beat most skip** *(§16)*

On a £2M transfer, an instant green check reads as reckless.

The brain doesn't trust a system that had no time to do the work — here, speed signals carelessness, not competence. Add a deliberate 150–250 ms beat before the confirmation and the same action feels considered.

---

**Standalone B — the equation / window** *(§7)*

Trust on a high-stakes action = the same green check + a 150–250 ms processing beat before it.

Below 150 ms: the user gets anxious. Above 250 ms: the app feels broken. Narrow window, real effect.

---

**Standalone C — stop / do** *(§9)*

Stop shipping instant success on irreversible actions.

Add the half-second. It's not friction — it's the system saying *we took this seriously.*

---

**Standalone D — old way → new way** *(§3)*

Optimistic UI, reconsidered:

Old way (consumer apps): show success instantly, do the work in the background. Speed signals competence.
New way (B2B finance): a deliberate beat on high-stakes actions. Speed signals the system didn't take you seriously.

Context decides.

---

**Standalone E — the comparison** *(§8)*

The green check that appears instantly < the one that takes half a second.

Counterintuitive. Also A/B tested. For high-stakes money movement, speed signals recklessness.

---

**Standalone F — document it once** *(§9)*

Stop re-deciding the delay per screen every sprint.

Put it in the design system: deliberate delay is the default for high-stakes interactions. Engineers implement it once. Everyone stops arguing about it.

---

**Standalone G — the audit move** *(§6)*

Walk the flow. Mark each action:

— high-stakes (payment, rebalance, irreversible) → add the 150–250 ms beat
— low-stakes (search, filter, nav) → speed still wins

The audit is the design work. Miss it and every button feels the same.

---

**Standalone H — the flip** *(§17 one-sentence opener)*

We spent a decade making interfaces faster.

One of the moves in 2026 is deliberately slowing them down in specific places. Not a bug. A trust mechanism.

---

**Standalone I — what actually built the trust** *(§10 contrarian lever)*

We got users to trust a high-stakes button more. What did it wasn't a faster response — it was a half-second of *nothing* before the green check.

## 5. Maturity as collective commitment {#maturity-collective}
> **Status:** draft · Twitter-only (LI angle covered by Idea 1)

**Standalone A — stages ladder** *(§15)*

Most design teams never get past stage two: good designers, but no shared language — so every call still rides on individual taste.

The jump to maturity isn't better designers. It's the whole org speaking one vocabulary: shared tokens as the source of truth.

---

**Standalone B — the comparison** *(§8)*

Shared language > better designers.

One strong designer in an org with no shared vocabulary is just one person's taste with good execution.

---

**Standalone C — the token point** *(§8)*

`#3358ff` is something a product manager gets confused by. `button-primary` isn't.

Tokens aren't a technical choice. They're the vocabulary that makes a design decision legible across the whole org.

---

**Standalone D — the jump isn't personal** *(§10)*

The jump from "decisions ride on individual taste" to "shared tokens are the source of truth" doesn't happen because the designer got better.

It happens because the org started sharing a language. Different problem, different fix.

---

**Standalone E — don't need / do need** *(§5)*

Raising design maturity is simple.

Don't need: more talented designers, a bigger team, a 200-page system.
Do need: tokens, a shared vocabulary, and product + engineering actually using it.

---

**Standalone F — the harder truth** *(§17)*

Design maturity is collective or it's nothing.

One strong designer in an org without shared language spends their week fighting for things nobody else can name — winning some, losing others on gut. Maturity makes those fights unnecessary.

---

**Standalone G — the top of the ladder** *(§17 → concrete)*

The top maturity stage isn't "the designers got great."

It's the top rung: AI agents read the design system as structured data and build consistent interfaces on their own. The token system becomes the brief the AI reads. You can't skip to it — you get there by defining your tokens first.
