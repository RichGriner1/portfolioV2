# AI-built sites without leaving WordPress

The most-shared AI-coding demos right now are the ones that build a working app in eight minutes from a single prompt. They're real.

But they don't reach most of the people who would benefit from AI the most — the marketers, founders, and small operators who already run their business on WordPress and can't throw it out for a vibe-coded site without losing everything they've built. The demos are flashy. The part where AI actually plugs into a real business — without breaking the workflow that business already runs on — is the part nobody is showing.

WordPress runs roughly 40% of the web. The people on it aren't on it because they're behind. They're on it because their content lives there, their plugins work, their forms go to their CRM, and their editor is the one their team already knows. Every AI demo I've seen assumes you're starting fresh. So the WordPress operator watches, can't see a path from where they are, and stays put. The gap widens.

I've been working with a B2B brand strategy founder on his site. Sharp on positioning, technically curious, willing to try new tools — exactly the kind of person these demos are aimed at. He'd been burned once already. His last site got rebuilt on a modern JavaScript stack. SEO collapsed. Google stopped finding his pages. He's currently paying someone to move it back to WordPress. When I brought up using AI to improve his new site, his reaction was simple: "Not if it means leaving WordPress again."

That's the question nobody is answering. Not "how do I build a slick demo with AI?" — but "how do I let AI improve my site without losing the platform I trust?" The answer is smaller than you'd think. And as a designer, it's where I think I can actually help people.

## The shape of the answer

I found that using Astro as a front-end shell sits on top of WordPress.

Which means the visitor sees a custom-designed page — the kind a designer or programmer can build on Cursor or Claude. The marketer logs into the same WordPress admin they've always used and edits content the same way they always have. When they hit "Update," the page rebuilds and reflects the change. Two lanes that don't touch.

The reason this matters for SEO is simple. Astro spits out plain HTML. Google doesn't have to run any JavaScript to read it — the content is just there. That's exactly the problem my client's last site had. A "modern stack" can quietly lose to plain HTML on the one metric most marketers actually care about: can anyone find the page?

## Why this is the unlock for non-technical people

Because the shell is separate from the content, AI can iterate on it freely. My job becomes improving the design as the tools get better. When a new idea is worth trying, I try it in the shell — and I can use AI to move faster doing it.

The other half is brand. Most AI-generated stuff looks generic because it's built against a generic prompt. But if you set the visual rules first — colors, fonts, spacing — and ask AI to work inside those rules, the output stays on-brand by default. Clients don't have to hope the AI guessed their brand right. It's already baked in. That's what makes iteration feel safe instead of scary.

## What I'd tell another designer or a non-technical operator

- **Don't abandon WordPress to use AI. Add to it.** The interesting work isn't on the platform. It's on the front-end that sits in front of it.
- **Plain HTML out-ranks JavaScript-heavy sites.** "Modern" isn't always search-friendly. Pre-built HTML is what Google reads cleanly on the first request.
- **Set your visual rules before letting AI generate anything.** That's how brand consistency survives iteration. Generic prompts produce generic output; clear rules make the output yours.
- **Treat content and design as two lanes.** The marketer owns one. The designer owns the other. AI works in the designer's lane and never touches the marketer's.
- **The unlock for non-technical AI adoption isn't a better prompt.** It's better scaffolding. The prompt is the part everyone shows. The scaffolding is the part that actually ships.
