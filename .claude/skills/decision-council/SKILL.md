# Decision council

A single model answering a decision question tends to average its advisers into one balanced, agreeable, forgettable take. This skill forces the opposite: five genuinely different people argue, review each other blind, and one Chairman commits. The value is in the **conflict** — do not smooth it out.

## The one rule that makes this work

**Do not blend the advisers.** Each is a different person with a different lens, vocabulary, and blind spot. If two sections could be swapped without noticing, you have failed. Different *language*, different *priorities*, different *things they refuse to care about*. Stay in character even when an adviser is being unfair — that's the point of having five.

## Before you start

The council needs a real decision with real constraints. If the user's decision is vague ("should I grow my business?"), ask **1–2 sharp clarifiers first** — what's the actual choice, what's the constraint, what does "good" look like — then run the council. A council run on a fuzzy question produces fuzzy advisers.

## Run it in three steps. Do not skip any.

### STEP 1 — Five separate answers
Write a labeled section per adviser. They do not reference each other yet.

1. **THE CONTRARIAN** — Looks *only* for what fails. Does not balance, does not hedge, offers no upside. Every reason this is wrong, what breaks first, and the worst plausible outcome. If they're being one-sided, good.
2. **THE FIRST-PRINCIPLES THINKER** — Rips apart the user's stated assumptions. Asks what they'd do if the obvious framework/tool/playbook were forbidden. Strips the problem to fundamentals and rebuilds from nothing.
3. **THE EXPANSIONIST** — Finds the upside the user is under-weighting. Reasons about the asymmetric outcome if it works. What does the *bigger* version of this unlock that the current framing hides?
4. **THE OUTSIDER** — Knows nothing about the user's industry. Asks the dumb questions only an outsider asks. Surfaces the load-bearing assumptions insiders stopped noticing years ago.
5. **THE EXECUTOR** — Does not care about strategy. Cares about Monday morning. Names the exact next moves: the email to send, the conversation to have, the file to create, the decision to defer this week.

### STEP 2 — Anonymous peer review
Now each adviser reviews the **other four**, anonymized as **Response A / B / C / D**. Do not reveal which response belongs to which adviser — the reviewer must not know they're reading "the Contrarian." Each adviser **ranks the other four 1–4** on accuracy and insight, with **one paragraph** per review naming what that response got right and what it got wrong. Keep the anonymization real: an adviser critiques ideas, not personas.

### STEP 3 — The Chairman's call
The Chairman has read all five answers and all five reviews. Synthesize **one clear recommendation. No hedging. No "on one hand / on the other."** State exactly:
- **What the right decision actually is** (pick one)
- **The one strongest reason for it**
- **The one biggest risk to watch for**
- **The specific next step to take in the next 7 days**

**Keep the Chairman under 250 words. Sharper is better.** A Chairman who won't commit has wasted the other four advisers.

## Anti-patterns that kill a council
- **Consensus theater** — five advisers who quietly agree. If nobody in Step 1 contradicts anyone else, you blended them. Rerun.
- **A hedging Chairman** — "it depends," "both have merit," two options offered. The Chairman's job is to remove the choice, not restate it.
- **A fake Outsider** — an Outsider who clearly knows the industry is just another insider. Make them genuinely naive.
- **A strategic Executor** — the Executor drifts into vision. Drag them back to what happens before Friday.
- **Reviews that just praise** — Step 2 must find real fault. A ranking with no downgrade reason is filler.
