## Standalone tweets (6)

Each one stands on its own. Mix into the schedule across the two weeks after the thread drops. Order isn't fixed.

---

**Standalone A — the diagnosis and the fix**

A cluttered enterprise product is usually just a product built one client request at a time, with nobody stepping back afterward to clean it up. The fix isn't fewer features. It's giving each one a defined role.

---

**Standalone B — the concrete case**

Our tool has two search boxes on one page. One switches between clients. One finds something inside a specific task. Same rough spot on the page. Different jobs. That's why both stay instead of getting combined into one search bar.

---

**Standalone C — what "clean and simple" actually costs**

Clean, simple apps usually only ask you to do one main thing. A chat app really just wants you to start a new conversation. A tool built for complicated business needs doesn't get that luxury. Stop measuring your product's calm against an app that only had one job to design for.

---

**Standalone D — vocabulary, not blueprint**

Clean apps teach you restraint. They don't teach you how to handle a product that has more to do than they did.

---

**Standalone E — the reference set**

Apps I compared ours to recently: Shopify. Clerk. Cursor. Notion. Stack AI. All calm on the surface. All built by a team that decided what not to include.

---

**Standalone F — the practice, stated plainly**

Before I add anything new to a client-facing tool, I ask one question: what job is this doing that nothing else already does? If the answer is "it looks nice," it doesn't ship yet.

## LinkedIn post (~215 words)

If your product feels cluttered next to the apps you admire, it's often because it has more jobs to do than they do.

I compared our financial planning tool to apps I admire: Shopify, Notion, Clerk. Every one of them feels calm and put together. Ours doesn't, and making ours look more like them wasn't going to fix that.

Here's why they feel calm: they mostly ask you to do one main thing. A chat app really just wants one thing from you, start a new conversation. Our tool doesn't get that luxury. It has to handle whatever a specific client needs on a given day, and every client needs something a little different. Each of those differences adds a setting nobody ever goes back and removes.

So I stopped trying to copy the cleaner apps. Now I ask one question before anything new goes in: what job is this doing that nothing else already does? Our tool has two search boxes on the same page. One switches between clients. One finds something inside a specific task. Different jobs, so both stay.

The question that cleans up a busy product isn't "does this look like the app I admire." It's "what job is this piece doing."
