## Twitter thread (8 tweets)

**Tweet 1 — hook**

The fastest way to make a dense product feel considered isn't fixing the main screen. It's the small moments around it.

---

**Tweet 2 — the reason, briefly**

Our wealth simulator is mostly dense tables and line graphs, the kind people work in all day. That's just what the content is, so I stopped trying to make it exciting and started looking elsewhere.

---

**Tweet 3 — the opportunity**

The pop-up windows. The screens you see when there's no data yet. Small details like how a button feels when you click it. That's where the extra care pays off.

---

**Tweet 4 — short punch**

That's the move: put the care where people notice it.

---

**Tweet 5 — the confirmed detail**

One thing already built into our demo: a small checkbox animation. Easy to miss. That's exactly the point.

---

**Tweet 6 — the next one**

One idea from a recent design session I want to try next: making a button visually push in when you click it, instead of just changing color. Small detail, real difference.

---

**Tweet 7 — why it matters**

None of this changes what the product does. It changes whether it feels considered, and that's what people remember.

---

**Tweet 8 — kicker**

The small moments are where a dense product gets to feel considered. Design them on purpose.

The small moments around a product's core screens are where design effort goes furthest: a pop-up, an empty screen, a tiny transition. That's on purpose, not an accident.

Where the extra care goes on a dense product: the pop-up windows, the empty screens, the small motion nobody talks about. Small footprint, real signal that someone cared.

Checkbox animations. Small button reactions. Subtle transitions. None of it is hard to build. All of it is worth building anyway.

## LinkedIn post (~185 words)

The fastest way to make a dense product feel considered isn't fixing the main screen. It's the small moments around it.

Our wealth simulator is mostly dense tables and line graphs, the kind people work in all day. That's just what the content is, so I stopped trying to make it exciting and started looking elsewhere: the pop-up windows, the screens you see when there's no data yet, the small details you barely notice, like how a button feels when you click it.

One thing already built into our demo: a small checkbox animation, easy to miss, and that's exactly the point. One idea from a recent design session I want to try next: making a button visually push in when you click it, instead of just changing color.

None of this changes what the product does. It changes whether it feels considered, and that's what people remember.

The small moments are where a dense product gets to feel considered. Design them on purpose.
