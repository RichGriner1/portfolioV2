# Modern UI in 2026: the research pass before I touched Figma

My boss handed me a vague task. *"Build a visual identity for our demos. Something more modern."* No persona, no buyer profile, no constraints.

I'm a sole designer at a fintech consultancy. The natural move would be to open Figma and start pulling references. I didn't. The first thing I did was research.

Desk research as step zero. Before the mood board. Before any concepts. Before any pixel.

I wanted to answer: What does modern UI mean in 2026?

The goal is a basic lay of the land before starting. Not thoroughness. When you skip this step, you end up reacting to references instead of using them. The mood board becomes a vote on taste. With a point of view, the mood board becomes a choice between trade-offs.

> **A note on scope.** I use "AI" broadly through this piece. AI features inside products (smart prefill, suggestions, summarization). AI-generated UI (generative layouts, components built on the fly). AI-assisted design tools (Figma AI, Claude Code, Paper). The scope is broader than chat products. Most of the patterns below apply across all of them — the differences show up where they show up.

## Step zero: why desk research before anything else

Most of the design conversation about AI right now is "AI is fast, designers need to keep up." That's true but speed is now the baseline, not the advantage. What's left is **identity** — the part the AI can't make up for you, because it's a story it doesn't have access to yet. Without a story, the AI fills the gap with its trained-in defaults, and every product looks like the same Tailwind-shadcn finish.

So for a visual identity refresh, the first job is writing down what your product stands for in a way the AI tools can read. Fonts come after. The typographic posture. The gradient policy. The negative-value convention. The dark-mode stance. The motion vocabulary. All the things people used to skip defining are now the difference. Horizontal spacing within a section versus between two stacked sections. The distance from nav to content. AI will guess these for you if you don't write them down. Generically.

You can't write that brief without context. And you can't know the context without researching.

## The problem behind the brief

The brief was vague because the conversation behind it was vague. Most design decisions at a small fintech consultancy without a written design language end up as preferences. The boss likes a serif this week. Last month it was something else. Patterns someone fights for one quarter feel obvious the next. The decisions ride on individual taste, not shared principles. That works when there's one product to ship and one taste to please. It breaks at five.

The research gave me a name for what I was running into. Multiple sources framed *design maturity* as a measure of how widely the design language is shared — not how skilled the designers are. Strong designers in a place without shared vocabulary stay at *Managed* maturity. The same designers in an org where product, engineering, and leadership all speak some design language jump to *Defined* or *Optimized*. The change is collective, not personal.

That's the bet behind this refresh. The fonts and colors are the visible part. The deeper move is introducing vocabulary the whole team can use when deciding about the interface, without re-litigating taste every time. Tokens are the substrate for that conversation — `button-primary` is something a product manager can argue about; `#3358ff` isn't.

A second concrete problem the research surfaced: tool dispersion. The Velvetum 2026 audit looked at a 14-designer team running on 8.2 active tools. Consolidating to 4.2 — Figma, Midjourney, Figma AI, Storybook, Code Connect — bought them 38% more productivity and dropped onboarding from 14 days to 4. Each new tool has a hidden cost. The question to ask is what closes when one opens.

## The shift: from layout to intent

The cleanest reframe across the sources I went through is UI used to be navigation: a tree of menus the user clicks through to find what they need. UI in 2026 is intent: the interface recognizes what the user is trying to accomplish and brings the relevant tools forward.

The four user intents — informational, navigational, commercial, transactional — aren't new. What's new is treating them as the *entry point* of the IA, not a downstream analytics framing. Intent-driven dashboards help users spot what matters, understand what changed, and know what to do next. The tree is still there. It's secondary.

For a B2B fin-tech, this matters more than it does for consumer fintech. The advisor isn't browsing. They're working a client file with a specific outcome in mind. The IA should let them say what they're trying to do, and the layout should reorganize the diagnostic around that goal. Not by rearranging the whole dashboard — that breaks spatial memory and reads as creepy — but by surfacing the right modules in a known slot.

This is where the research validated a move I'd already made. Our existing platform structure has a fixed set of slots: page actions, section actions, filter rows, modal previews. The structure stays the same. The content inside the slots changes based on what the advisor's working on. That's how shared brand identity survives in a world where every interface is partly generative — **the patterns carry the brand**.

## Friction as a feature

The most counter-intuitive thing we found in research is also the most actionable. For a decade, engineers chased instant response on every interaction. Designers in 2026 are deliberately adding delays back in.

The example I came back to: a designer (Emil Kowalski) ran an A/B on two submit buttons for a high-stakes action. One showed a green check the millisecond it was clicked. The other showed a half-second processing animation, then the same green check. Users overwhelmingly trusted the delayed version.

The mechanism is **perceived reliability**. For a high-stakes action — authorizing a large payment, moving funds, rebalancing a client portfolio — the user's brain literally does not believe a system that responds too fast had time to do the work. The optimistic UI pattern (show success instantly, do the work in the background) actively damages trust in this context.

The window is small: 150–250 milliseconds. Long enough for the brain to register that something happened. Short enough that the app doesn't feel sluggish. Below 150 ms triggers anxiety; above 250 ms feels broken.

I'm adding this to the project as a checklist item. Walking the user flow, marking the moments where a deliberate delay earns trust — payment authorization, portfolio rebalancing, document generation — and the moments where speed still wins (search, filter, navigation). Not a blanket rule. A judgment call per interaction. The same audit surfaces moments of delight: small character beats where the demo can carry personality without slowing the user down.

## What "trust" actually means here

Two pieces of the research sharpened what "trust" actually means in this kind of product.

Stan Vision's 2026 fintech piece defines trust in financial products as **transparency + consistency + responsiveness**. What that looks like in practice:

- **Predict, but always announce.** When the system prefills a transfer, it says why. *"Based on your last three payroll transfers to this vendor, we filled this form for you. Tap to clear."* Silent prefill reads as surveillance. Announced prefill reads as competence.
- **Friction where it earns.** The 150–250 ms processing beat on high-stakes actions, from the previous section. Trust comes from the system signaling it took the action seriously.
- **Biometrics as handshake.** Face ID, fingerprint, voice — they stopped being security measures and became an emotional cue. *"We know it's you, your environment is secure, let's proceed."*

The other anchor I kept coming back to was Don Norman's three layers of emotional design. **Visceral** is the first-impression reaction. **Behavioral** is how it feels during use. **Reflective** is how it sits with the user afterwards. An interface that wins only the visceral layer falls apart in use. A B2B advisor surface has to land all three layers because the user comes back to the same product daily, for years — the reflective layer is where the relationship lives.

The mode-based UI trend extending Norman's reflective layer — *Morning mode*, *Focus mode*, *Evening mode* — isn't a fit for an advisor product yet. But the underlying point holds. The interface should change with the user's context, not just their permissions.

## Raw aesthetics: expressive minimalism

The visual trend that surprised me most: B2B fintech is moving toward what some sources call **raw aesthetics**. Visible grids. Monospaced numerals. Ledger-style number formatting. Wireframe logic. The example everyone cites is Crezco's payment product — square payment nodes connected by direct lines, looking more like a control panel than a SaaS dashboard.

When I first saw this I pushed back on it. It looks unfinished. It looks like a 1990s wireframe with the design phase skipped. My initial read was "designers calling lazy work a trend."

What changed my mind was thinking about the target user. A CFO moving millions of pounds doesn't want a bouncy onboarding flow. They want a high signal-to-noise ratio. A blueprint-like interface reads as operational logic. Competence. Serious infrastructure. The trust comes from the *absence* of decoration. From the message that the team building this is focused entirely on the mechanics of the money, not the chrome.

So the move is to recognize that for a B2B advisor product, restraint earns its keep — not to copy Crezco. The visual identity should support density without apologizing for it. The data is the product. The chrome should get out of the way.

This dovetails with what's happening to glassmorphism. The early-2020s version (frosted glass on everything) is dead because it failed accessibility checks and competed with content. The 2026 version is Linear's correction — they call it **anti-liquid glass**. They kept the depth signal (so the user knows they're looking at an overlay) but stripped out the refractive distortion that made text underneath unreadable. Glass on chrome. Solid backgrounds on data. That's the rule.

## TokenOps: the master map for generative UI

This was the section of the research I needed least, because I'd already built it. But it gave me language for what I'd already built.

The argument: if AI is going to generate UI on the fly (a custom slider control here, a one-off form there), it needs a single source of truth for every visual decision. Not a Figma file with hex codes. A semantic system where `button-primary-bg` means something to both a human engineer and an AI agent. The agent reads the design system as structured logical data, generates an interface, and the interface is automatically on brand because every value flows through the same tokens.

The industry is calling this **TokenOps** — the operational discipline around semantic tokens. Assigned ownership. Semantic versioning. Quarterly audits. Dependency mapping.

The useful piece was the maturity model. Five stages: Ad hoc → Managed → Defined → Optimized → Adaptive. Tokens become the source of truth at the **Defined** stage. The highest stage, **Adaptive**, is characterized by AI agents being able to read the design system as structured data — through a Model Context Protocol server or similar — and use it to generate consistent interfaces.

Our system already runs at Defined to Optimized. We have a three-tier token hierarchy. Components only reference semantic tokens. Tokens carry their own dark/light mode logic. We have a design.md that codifies the architecture. The visual-identity refresh is the move toward Adaptive.

I wrote that in my notes during the research, almost as a self-validation: *AI needs a master map. That's what I built.* The 2026 framing makes that move legible to non-designers in a way "we have a token system" doesn't.

## Tools to customize, not customization forced on them

The phrase I kept coming back to. Not because it's catchy, but because it's the practical version of every accessibility, personalization, and AI-control conversation in the research.

The pattern is everywhere now. Dashboards let advisors compose their own layout from modules instead of shipping a "smart default" the AI silently rearranges. Reduced motion is a visible toggle in onboarding, not a hidden setting six menus deep. Glass surfaces respect `prefers-reduced-transparency`. AI prefilled fields announce what they did and offer a one-tap override. *"Based on your last three payroll transfers to this vendor, we filled this form for you. Tap to clear."*

The pattern signals respect. The product gives you the tools and acknowledges you're the one driving. It works because it's the design version of consent. A B2B advisor surface in particular needs this — these users have professional responsibility for what the system suggests. Silent automation here isn't speed. It's a liability.

I'm marking this for the visual-identity work as a stance the demo should embody. Wherever there's automation, there's a visible override. Wherever there's a default, there's a way to change it. The demo should *show* this, not just have it.

## What I'm doing next

Five moves I'm adding to visual-identity projects: 

1. **Lead with the token-layer story.** The visual-identity refresh takes Coherence one step further — toward Adaptive maturity, where tokens are structured data the AI can read, modes compose, and accessibility validation lives at the token-creation layer. Direction A on the mood board is *"Adaptive Tokens"* — the visual identity emerges from the token architecture, not the other way around.

2. **Glass on chrome, not data.** Glassmorphism on nav, modals, callout cards. Body data on solid surfaces. Follow Linear's correction — keep the depth signal, drop the refractive distortion. Direction B is *"Quiet Glass"* — depth as a system signal, restrained.

3. **Friction as a feature.** Walk the user flow and mark every high-stakes action. Authorization, rebalancing, document generation, irreversible changes. Add a deliberate 150–250 ms processing beat. Document the rule in the design system so it's the default behavior, not a per-screen decision.

4. **Make AI legible without making it a chat.** When the system suggests a prefill, a layout, or a next step, it announces what it's doing and surfaces a one-tap override. Reasoning is grounded in the underlying data (the highlighted row, the similar past case) rather than a chat-style explanation. Confidence is shown as High/Medium/Low or n-best alternatives, never as a percentage. Numeric precision reads as false precision in finance.

5. **Phase the work.** The MVP is fonts and colors on the existing demo. Phase 2 is micro-interactions on the user flows. Phase 3 is generative layouts driven by user goals. The sequence matters. Skipping the visual layer to go straight to generative would mean every AI-built screen carries the trained-in aesthetic instead of an Afi one. Visual identity first, smarter layouts second.

## The kicker

The provocative thought from the research that I keep coming back to: if generative UI gets good enough, no two users see the exact same layout. Every interface is composed for the specific user's intent. So what happens to brand identity?

My answer (also from my own notes during the research, almost in real time): the brand identity is the **patterns**, not the pixels. The fact that page actions always live in the top-right corner. The fact that filters always sit above the table. The fact that high-stakes confirmations always carry a 200 ms processing beat. The fact that AI suggestions always live in the margin and never overwrite content. These patterns are the brand. The content inside them can be entirely user-specific without diluting it.

Creativity in 2026 has moved one level up the stack. From pushing pixels to designing patterns and experiences. The machine builds the screen. The designer builds the trust.

I needed to read for a few hours to be confident in that take. The mood board next week will be sharper because I did.
