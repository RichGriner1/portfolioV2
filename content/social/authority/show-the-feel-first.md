## Twitter thread (5 tweets)

**Tweet 1 — hook**

Show a client the feel your design adds up to, skip the raw materials, and you get a faster yes than any explanation could.

---

**Tweet 2 — anecdote**

A client I work with doesn't know what a mood board is, a loose collage of images and colors that sets a direction before any real design happens. Doesn't matter. Put one in front of them and the reaction is instant: this feels right, or it doesn't.

---

**Tweet 3 — the move**

So the raw references, the stack my team picks through to find a direction, stay internal. What the client sees is the distilled version: here's the feel we landed on, not the pile of screenshots that got us there.

---

**Tweet 4 — the why**

That's not about hiding the work. It's matching the artifact to the audience. People building the thing need the raw material. People paying for the outcome need to know it'll feel right before it exists.

---

**Tweet 5 — kicker**

Whatever you're building, the person deciding whether to trust it rarely needs the construction explained. Show them the feel first. Save the mechanics for the people who build it.

## Standalone tweets (7)

Each one stands on its own. Mix into the schedule across the two weeks after the thread drops. Order isn't fixed.

---

**Standalone A — the vivid image**

Put a finished mood board in front of someone who's never made one, and they don't ask how it works. They just know instantly if it's right.

---

**Standalone B — the method as steps**

Presenting design work to someone outside the craft:

1. Skip the drafts and dead ends.
2. Skip the raw references.
3. Show the feel it adds up to.

That's the whole deliverable, for them.

---

**Standalone C — the setup/payoff**

The team building a design goes through a lot of raw material to find a direction: references, drafts, dead ends. None of that belongs in front of the client. What they see is the one thing it adds up to, does this feel right for them.

---

**Standalone D — the permission-giving line**

A client saying "I don't really get design, I just know it when I see it" isn't a knowledge gap you need to fix. That's the correct way for them to evaluate it.

---

**Standalone E — the contrast**

Walking a client through your process earns respect from other builders. Showing them the finished feel is what earns the decision.

---

**Standalone F — the wider frame**

This isn't only a design thing. A lawyer's client doesn't want the case law. They want to know they'll win. Show the person paying the outcome, save the mechanics for the people who build it.

---

**Standalone G — the two-deliverable rule**

Two different deliverables live in every project: the raw material, for the people building it, and the felt outcome, for the people paying for it. Keep them separate and both audiences get exactly what they need.

## LinkedIn post

Here's a thing I think is true: the person paying for a design almost never needs to see how it was made. They need to feel, fast, whether it's right.

A client I work with doesn't know what a mood board is, a loose collage of images and colors that sets a direction before any real design happens. Doesn't matter. Put one in front of them and the reaction is instant: this feels right, or it doesn't.

So the raw material, the pile of references and drafts and half-finished ideas my team picks through to find a direction, stays internal. What reaches the client is the one distilled thing it points to. Not the process. The feel.

That's not about hiding the work. It's matching the deliverable to the person receiving it. Builders need the raw material to build with. The person paying for the outcome needs to know, before it exists, that it'll feel right.

Whatever you're building, the person deciding whether to trust it rarely needs the construction explained. Show them the feel first, and save the mechanics for the people who build it.
