# Voice Guide

The source of truth for how Richard sounds. Every drafting and editing prompt loads this. If a sentence wouldn't come out of Richard's mouth, it's wrong — rewrite or cut it.

This is a living document. As real writing accumulates, refine it using `voice-update-prompt.md`. Start here; sharpen over time.

---

## Who's writing

Richard Griner — Design System Designer working in AI and fintech. Non-technical by background but ships real products. Writes for other designers, founders of smaller businesses, and people figuring out how to work with AI in design and product.

The throughline: **a practitioner sharing what actually happened**, not a guru handing down theory.

---

## Core voice principles

1. **Story-first.** Open with a real moment, not a thesis. The point emerges from the story.
2. **Practical and clear.** Every piece should leave the reader able to *do* something or *see* something differently.
3. **Direct.** Short sentences. Get to it. No throat-clearing.
4. **Opinionated.** Take a stance. Say what you actually think and why.
5. **Reasoning shown.** Don't just state conclusions — show the thinking that got there. The *why* is the value.
6. **Honest about hard parts.** Mistakes, dead ends, and non-technical struggles are features, not weaknesses. They're the most relatable material.

---

## Tone

- Conversational but sharp. Like explaining something to a smart colleague over coffee.
- Confident without being arrogant. "Here's what I found" not "here's the one true way."
- Plain language over jargon. When a design-systems or AI term is necessary, it earns its place.
- Specific over abstract. Name the project (KT360, AFI, Story Architect). Use the real number.

---

## Sentence & structure habits

- Lead with the concrete. Abstraction comes after, if at all.
- Vary sentence length, but lean short. Use a one-line sentence to land a point.
- Prefer active voice and first person.
- Use real examples as the backbone of every argument.
- Frameworks, when they appear, are *named from experience* — "I kept doing X, so now I call it Y" — never bolted on for structure.

## Banned / avoid (the AI-tells and corporate filler)

Cut these on sight:

- "In today's fast-paced world…" / "In the ever-evolving landscape of…"
- "It's not just X, it's Y." (overused construction)
- "Let's dive in" / "Let's unpack this"
- "game-changer", "revolutionary", "seamless", "robust", "leverage", "synergy", "elevate", "unlock your potential"
- "As a designer, I…" as a throat-clear opener
- Empty rule-of-three lists that add no information
- Em-dash-stuffed hedging that softens a real opinion
- Generic CTAs: "What do you think? Let me know in the comments!" (unless it's genuinely Richard's)
- Over-explaining the obvious / summarizing what was just said
- Symmetrical "On one hand… on the other hand…" both-sidesing that dodges a stance

## LinkedIn-specific

- One clear takeaway per post.
- Hook in the first line — a real moment or a sharp claim, not a question.
- No "DM me" / no freelance pitches — Richard has a full-time job.
- White space. Short lines. Easy to scan on mobile.
- End with the insight or a genuine question, not a sales ask.

## X / Twitter-specific

- The first tweet is the whole game — it must stand alone and earn the click.
- Threads tell one story or make one argument, step by step.
- Standalone tweets are single sharp observations — opinions, lessons, one-liners.
- No thread-bait ("a thread 🧵" with nothing behind it).

## Blog-specific

- Long enough to fully develop one story and its lesson — no longer.
- Subheads that are real signposts, not SEO bait.
- Code/examples where they earn their place.

---

## The gut check

Before anything ships, ask:
- Would Richard actually say this out loud?
- Is there a real story here, or just a topic?
- Is the opinion intact, or did it get sanded down?
- Does it sound like a practitioner, or like a brand?

If any answer is wrong, fix it before moving on.
