# Clarity & length: does it parse, is it developed?

The second half of the substance gate — the "unclear / too short / forced" failures specifically.

## Clarity — does every sentence parse on first read?
- **One idea per sentence.** Two ideas joined by "and" → split them.
- **Concrete before abstract.** Lead with the specific thing (a number, a proper noun, a real example), then the principle — not the reverse.
- **No throat-clearing.** Cut the wind-up ("It's worth noting that…", "At the end of the day…", generic framing openers). Start at the point.
- **The read-aloud test.** If a sentence makes you stumble reading it out loud, it's unclear — rewrite. If a claim would make a skeptical colleague go "…okay, but what do you *actually* mean?", it's vague — make it specific.

## Length — is it developed enough? (the "too short" failure)
- **Too short = asserted, not earned.** A post that states a takeaway but never shows the reasoning, the example, or the mechanism leaves the reader with a claim they can't use. Develop the one point: what happened, why it matters, what to do with it.
- **The floor is "fully developed," not a word count.** Long enough to develop *one* point and its lesson — and no longer. If a section is one line where the idea needs three to land, expand it. If it's three paragraphs where one lands, cut.
- **Rough platform sense** (adapt to the actual target): a short social post still needs a hook + the point + why it matters — not just a hook. Long-form runs as long as the single story needs. A one-liner that's really a headline for a missing body is "too short."
- **Symptom:** if you can't apply the screenshot test because there's "not enough there," it's too short.
- **Over-concision is a regression, not tightening.** Cutting is good *until* it removes the concrete that makes the point land — a proper noun, the thesis-support paragraph the title promises, the best specific example/anecdote. If a "tightening" pass deletes the material that proved the claim (leaving a leaner but now-generic or unearned piece), that's a step backward: flag it and restore the specific, don't wave it through because the word count dropped. Lean means *one topic, no filler* — not *stripped of evidence*.

## Forced — the angle that only exists to sound clever
The most common quality failure after "too short." Tells:
- **Over-symmetry.** "X isn't a Y, it's a Z." / "Not just A, but B." At most one per piece, and only when the turn is *true and specific*, not just balanced. If it's decorative, cut it.
- **Anthropomorphized cuteness** ("your color system is lying to the user") — flag unless it's carrying a precise point.
- **Could-apply-to-anything** — a line that would fit under any post in the field is filler dressed as insight.
- **Profound-sounding, content-free** ("Design isn't decoration, it's decision.") — the test: would it survive being said aloud to a skeptical colleague, or would they ask what you mean? If it needs the construction to stand, cut it.
- **Three-item lists where two would do** — LLMs default to three; if the third is filler, drop it.
- **Unearned jargon** — a term used to sound expert rather than to be precise. Replace with the plain, specific word.

## Verdicts
- Unclear sentence → *revise* with the specific line and a plainer rewrite direction.
- Too short / underdeveloped → *revise*: "asserts the takeaway but doesn't develop it — add the mechanism/example."
- Forced angle → *revise*: quote the line, name why it's forced, suggest cutting or grounding it.
- Clean → pass to the language lens (`voice-griner` / `afi-redaccion`).
