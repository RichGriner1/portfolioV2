## Twitter thread (8 tweets)

**Tweet 1 — hook**

Before you shrink a good idea because "it'll take too long to build," check if that's still true.

---

**Tweet 2 — what changed**

We started building one of our products from scratch, instead of adding onto the years-old code it used to run on. On top of that, AI tools make the actual building faster.

---

**Tweet 3 — what that meant**

Before, a complicated feature wasn't worth designing in detail. Building it would have taken the engineers too long. Now that building is faster, designing it in full is worth doing.

---

**Tweet 4 — short punch**

That's what changed. Not what I could imagine, what was worth drawing.

---

**Tweet 5 — what it exposed**

It also showed me something about myself: I was still shrinking my designs out of habit, even after the reason for doing that was gone.

---

**Tweet 6 — the concrete case**

Example: we have a complicated flow for importing financial data, full of special rules and exceptions. I used to simplify it before anyone else saw it. This time I designed the whole thing.

---

**Tweet 7 — the handoff**

Then I handed it to the team that builds the product, in stages: here's the simple version, build this first. Here's the fuller version, that's what we're building toward. Nothing gets lost. It just gets built in order.

---

**Tweet 8 — kicker**

If you're scaling back an idea because "that's not realistic to build," check whether that's still true, or whether it's just the reason you used to have.

**Standalone A — the old habit, named**

## LinkedIn post (~200 words)

Before you shrink a good idea because "it'll take too long to build," check if that's still true.

We started building one of our products from scratch, instead of adding onto the years-old code it used to run on. On top of that, AI tools make the actual building faster. Before, a complicated feature wasn't worth designing in detail, because building it would have taken the engineers too long. Now that building is faster, designing it in full is worth doing.

That combination didn't just speed things up. It showed me I was still shrinking my designs out of habit, even after the reason for doing that was gone.

One example: we have a complicated flow for importing financial data, full of special rules and exceptions. I used to simplify it before anyone else saw it. This time I designed the whole thing, and handed it to the team that builds the product in stages: here's the simple version to build first, here's the fuller version to build toward later.

If you're scaling back an idea because "that's not realistic to build," check whether that's still true, or whether it's just the reason you used to have.
