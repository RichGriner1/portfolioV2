## Twitter thread (5 tweets)

**Tweet 1 — hook**

Your real taste isn't what you'd argue for in a meeting.

It's what you keep circling without planning to, when you're picking on your own.

---

**Tweet 2 — anecdote**

I was picking design references on Mobin, one component at a time: buttons, cards, menus, tables. I wasn't trying to be consistent. I kept landing on the same handful of apps anyway.

---

**Tweet 3 — the plan**

Next step: put my picks next to a collaborator's, each made without seeing the other's list, and let whatever overlaps set the direction, before either of us opens a design file.

---

**Tweet 4 — the mechanism**

Here's why that beats agreeing in a meeting: two people who work together will nod along to plenty they don't really believe. Picking blind, separately, cuts that out. Whatever survives without a conversation first is the overlap worth trusting.

---

**Tweet 5 — kicker**

You don't have to know why a design works to have real taste. You have to find out, early, whether the person building it with you has the same taste, before a finished screen forces the conversation.

## Standalone tweets (7)

Each one stands on its own. Mix into the schedule across the two weeks after the thread drops. Order isn't fixed.

---

**Standalone A — the self-convergence**

I picked references for several different components without trying to stay consistent. I kept landing on the same handful of apps anyway. That's worth more than anything I'd say out loud about my own taste.

---

**Standalone B — the method as steps**

Taste-check for a founder and a designer, or two co-founders, before any pixels get made:

1. Pick references alone.
2. Don't compare until both lists exist. Early comparing just means whoever talks first wins.
3. Whatever overlaps without discussion is your real direction.

---

**Standalone C — the plan, stated plainly**

My collaborator and I are about to compare our design references, picked completely separately, for the same product. Neither of us has seen the other's list yet. Whatever overlaps becomes the direction, no matter which of us picked it first.

---

**Standalone D — the meeting-agreement myth**

A meeting full of nodding heads tells you people can agree in front of each other. It doesn't tell you what either of them likes when nobody's watching, which is the only version of taste worth building a product on.

---

**Standalone E — the caution**

Fastest way to ruin a taste-check: talk about what you like before either of you has picked anything. Now you're not finding shared taste. You're finding out who talks first.

---

**Standalone F — the failure mode**

Most disagreements about how something should look don't show up on day one. They show up once there's a finished screen to react to, and by then it's expensive to fix and it feels personal. Check for shared taste before that point exists, not after.

---

**Standalone G — the rare-signal frame**

The same app showing up as my pick for the button, then the card, then the menu, when I wasn't trying to be consistent, is rare enough to notice. Things you didn't force are the closest thing design taste has to real data.

## LinkedIn post

A collaborator and I are each picking design references for the same product, on Mobin, working through it one component at a time: buttons, cards, menus, tables.

Going category by category, I kept landing on the same handful of apps. I wasn't trying to be consistent. It just kept happening.

That's the part worth trusting. Not what I'd argue for if you put me in a room and asked me to defend a style. What I keep circling on my own, unprompted, across things that have nothing to do with each other.

Next step: put my picks next to my collaborator's, each made without seeing the other's list, and let whatever overlaps set the direction, before either of us opens a design file.

Here's the check, if you're building anything visual with a partner, co-founder, or hired designer, and neither of you can point to "why" a design works:

1. Pick references alone first, split by the pieces you're deciding: the button, the card, the nav. Notice what you keep circling without meaning to. That's your real taste, not what you'd say out loud.
2. Compare only after both lists exist. Comparing early just means the louder opinion wins early.
3. Wherever you both converged without trying, that's your direction. Not the final design, the direction you can now defend.

You don't need a design vocabulary to know what you like. You need a way to find out if the person building it with you likes the same thing, before you're both staring at a finished screen wondering why it looks like a compromise.
